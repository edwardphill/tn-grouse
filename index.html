Ruffed Grouse (Bonasa umbellus) in Tennessee: a field report on range, decline, and contemporary habitat

Edward Hill · edward@fisherguiding.com
Abstract

The ruffed grouse (Bonasa umbellus) reaches the southern limit of its breeding range in Tennessee. Once distributed across East and most of Middle Tennessee, naturally occurring populations are now restricted to the Cumberland Plateau and the higher ridges and mountains of East Tennessee. We synthesize Christmas Bird Count detections across ~46 active Tennessee circles over 1966–2024 — within which ruffed grouse were recorded on only nine occasions, at six circles — together with TWRA Watchable Wildlife range and harvest figures, the Eastern Grouse Working Group's 2020 regional decline assessment (−71% in the Southern Appalachians since 1989), eBird observations from 2016 onward, and recent field sightings to characterize the bird's contemporary distribution and identify the most promising candidate sites for further survey on the Cumberland Plateau.

1.

Historical and contemporary range

The species' historical Tennessee range covered East and most of Middle Tennessee, reaching west into the Western Highland Rim. Naturally occurring populations are now restricted to the Cumberland Plateau and the higher ridges and mountains of East Tennessee.

The ruffed grouse breeding range extends from central Alaska across forested Canada and south along the Appalachian Mountains to northern Georgia; Tennessee sits at the southern edge of that range. TWRA's effort to reestablish a population on the Western Highland Rim has been described by the agency as "marginally successful."

"The Ruffed Grouse is an uncommon permanent resident in East Tennessee and locally uncommon in Middle Tennessee." TWRA Watchable Wildlife
2.

Christmas Bird Count detections, 1966–2024

The Audubon Christmas Bird Count (CBC) is a citizen-science survey conducted on a fixed 15-mile-diameter circle, by the same observers, every December. Approximately 46 CBC circles have been active in Tennessee at some point between 1966 and 2024, covering West, Middle, and East Tennessee. Across these decades of state-wide counting, ruffed grouse were recorded on just nine occasions, at six circles — all of them on the Cumberland Plateau or in the high ridges and mountains of East Tennessee. The remaining 40 circles never recorded a ruffed grouse on any count, consistent with the species' contraction from its historical range.

3.

Regional decline

The Eastern Grouse Working Group's 2020 report, the most rigorous regional assessment available, found that ruffed grouse declined by at least 50% across the eastern United States over the prior two decades, and by 71% in the Southern Appalachians since 1989.

The primary driver is loss of young forest. National Forests in the Southern Appalachians currently contain approximately 1.3% young forest (trees 0–20 years old), well below what grouse require. The species depends on a mosaic of forest ages within a single home range, and that mosaic has been largely smoothed away by even-aged maturation and fire suppression.

TWRA describes the in-state population as "stable," and this may hold locally on the Plateau. However, stability at a depleted baseline is qualitatively different from a healthy population. The 1989–90 harvest of approximately 50,000 birds by 21,000 hunters (TN Watchable Wildlife) is unlikely to be matched under current conditions.

"Ruffed grouse have declined 71% since 1989 in the Southern Appalachians. At the same time, National Forests in the Southern Appalachians contain only 1.3% young forests." Ruffed Grouse Society, Southern Appalachian Region

Population cycles relevant to interpretation

The hare cycle. In the northern range, grouse populations rise and fall with snowshoe hares on an approximately 8–11-year cycle as predators switch prey. This cycle is weak or absent in Tennessee, but predator pressure on small, fragmented populations remains a factor.

The forest succession cycle. A clearcut or controlled burn opens habitat that becomes prime grouse range at 10–20 years post-disturbance and ages out by approximately year 30. Without active management, the habitat clock runs down.

4.

Habitat requirements

The species requires three forest age classes within a single home range; the highest-quality habitat occurs where these classes intersect (the "activity center").

The 10–20 year class

Densely stocked young hardwoods or mixed stands with stems of roughly wrist- to arm-thickness. Understory cover that permits a hen with chicks to move through while restricting raptor stoops. Rhododendron, mountain laurel, fruit brambles, and blueberry are indicator species.

The 5–15 year class (early successional)

Brushy seedling-sapling stands provide brood habitat. Field edges and openings with herbaceous cover support the insect populations that young birds require during their high-energy first weeks. In Tennessee, this stage is the most scarce and is actively managed on RGS focal areas.

The 25+ year class (mast and drumming)

Mature, predominantly deciduous forest with oak, hickory, and beech provides hard mast through fall and winter. Drumming logs, typically downed trunks of ≥10 inches diameter, are used as display sites in successive springs.

Comparative observations from other grouse states

Minnesota — the activity center concept. Northern-range research frames grouse habitat as three age classes (0–10 years, 10–25 years, 25+ years) that must coexist within a single home range. The intersection of the three age classes is termed the activity center. The practical implication for Tennessee scouting is that young forest alone is insufficient; the young forest must abut both early-successional cover and mature mast-producing trees.

Vermont — winter softwoods. In the northern range, grouse dive into snow roosts on cold nights. In Vermont and at lower latitudes, when snow cover is lacking the birds shift to softwood stands (hemlock, spruce, pine) for protective cover and insulation. Tennessee winters rarely deliver deep, persistent snow, so this is relevant: shortleaf pine pockets and hemlock draws adjacent to hardwood feeding areas are probable winter refuges and worth noting on scouting maps.

Reading the ground

Edges where age classes meet are productive, particularly where a 10–20 year stand abuts a 25+ stand near water. Logging roads should be walked slowly. Pausing periodically (Hill, J.) pressures held birds to move and reveal themselves with peeps; the pause itself induces movement. Drumming runs from mid-April through mid-May and is the single best indicator of a holding population.

5.

Candidate field sites

Four candidate areas on or near the Cumberland Plateau, ranked by current promise; all are within 2–3 hours of Nashville.

Recommendation

Primary recommendation: Catoosa WMA

Catoosa offers approximately 5,000 acres of Plateau hardwoods, contiguous with approximately 4,000 acres at Lone Mountain State Forest, and an active Ruffed Grouse Society burn-and-cut project addressing the regional young-forest deficit. Forum-reported sightings near Lancing fall just to the north. For a single-weekend survey, this is the recommended starting point.

Site 1 — primary

Catoosa WMA

5,000 ac · Cumberland Plateau · 2h 20m from Nashville

Adjacent to Lone Mountain State Forest (~9,000 acres of contiguous habitat combined). Hosts a Ruffed Grouse Society habitat burn-and-cut project initiated in 2022. Forum reports of birds nearby in Lancing.

Active young-forest management is the single strongest predictor of grouse on the Plateau, and Catoosa has the most of it currently underway.

Site 2

Lone Mountain State Forest

4,000 ac · Cumberland Plateau · 2h 45m from Nashville

Contiguous with Catoosa and part of the same RGS Dynamic Forest Restoration Block project. Treat as a single survey unit with Catoosa; cross-reference unit boundaries before opening day.

Site 3

Standing Stone State Forest & WMA

11,000 ac · Cumberland Plateau · 2h 10m from Nashville

Historical timber cutting and fire have produced a mixed-age mosaic. Stand-age distribution: 38% in 81+ years, 36% in 61–80 years, 11% in 41–60 years, 7% in 21–40 years, 7% under 20 years. The under-20 fraction is limited but present; the productive search area is where it abuts older stands.

Grouse presence currently unconfirmed; promising on paper, requires ground-truthing.

Site 4

Pickett State Forest & WMA

8,000 ac · Cumberland Plateau · 2h 45m from Nashville

Northern Plateau, near the Kentucky line. Requires scouting; potentially holds birds based on habitat type and adjacency to managed lands. Lowest data confidence of the four sites.

6.

Season regulations and field tactics

Season (2025–26)

October 11, 2025 – February 28, 2026. Daily bag limit: 3 birds. Statewide.

License

TN resident annual hunting license required. Confirm current pricing and any WMA-specific permits on the TWRA site before going.

Field tactics

  • Walk edges where age classes meet. Pure mature timber holds drummers but typically not flushes.
  • Pause every 30–40 yards; standing still pressures held birds to move and reveal themselves with peeps (Hill, J.).
  • Walk logging roads in early morning for gravel and grit; birds work road edges at first light.
  • Listen for drumming in spring (mid-April through mid-May) to identify cover for the following fall.
