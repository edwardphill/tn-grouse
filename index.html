§ 01 — Range

Once across half the state. Now a thin crescent in the east.

The bird's historical Tennessee range covered East and most of Middle Tennessee, reaching west into the Western Highland Rim. Today, naturally occurring populations are restricted to the Cumberland Plateau and the high ridges and mountains of East Tennessee.

The Ruffed Grouse breeding range extends from central Alaska across forested Canada and south along the Appalachian Mountains to northern Georgia — and Tennessee sits at the southern edge of that range. TWRA's effort to reestablish a population on the Western Highland Rim has been, in the agency's own language, "marginally successful."

"The Ruffed Grouse is an uncommon permanent resident in East Tennessee and locally uncommon in Middle Tennessee." — TWRA Watchable Wildlife
§ 02 — Numbers Over Time

Six bird-count circles. Six decades. Nine grouse.

The Audubon Christmas Bird Count (CBC) is a citizen-science survey conducted on a fixed 15-mile-diameter circle, by the same observers, every December. Six circles in East and Middle Tennessee have run for decades — and across roughly 300 station-years of counting, grouse turned up just nine times.

Drag the year slider to see which circles detected birds and which didn't. A dashed bar means the count wasn't conducted that year. CBC counts are winter data — they undercount a forest game bird that doesn't flock, but they're a reasonable index of presence vs. absence across decades.

§ 03 — The Long Decline

A bird whose floor keeps dropping.

The Eastern Grouse Working Group's 2020 report — the most rigorous regional assessment available — found that ruffed grouse declined by at least 50% across the eastern United States over the prior two decades, and by 71% in the Southern Appalachians since 1989.

The primary driver is loss of young forest. National Forests in the Southern Appalachians currently contain only about 1.3% young forest (trees 0–20 years old) — far below what grouse need. The bird requires a mosaic of forest ages within a single home range, and that mosaic has largely been smoothed away by even-aged maturation and fire suppression.

TWRA still describes the in-state population as "stable," and that may be true locally on the Plateau. But stability at a depleted floor is a different thing than a healthy population. The 1989–90 harvest of ~50,000 birds by 21,000 hunters is unlikely to be matched today.

"Ruffed grouse have declined 71% since 1989 in the Southern Appalachians. At the same time, National Forests in the Southern Appalachians contain only 1.3% young forests." — Ruffed Grouse Society, Southern Appalachian Region

Two cycles to know

The 10-year hare cycle: in the northern range, grouse populations rise and fall with snowshoe hares on a roughly 8–11-year cycle as predators switch prey. This cycle is weak or absent in TN — but predator pressure on small, fragmented populations is still a factor.

The forest succession cycle: a clearcut or burn opens habitat that becomes prime grouse range at 10–20 years, then ages out by year 30. Without active management, the habitat clock runs down.

§ 04 — Habitat

What grouse need. Where to look.

Three forest age classes inside a single home range: the bird needs the activity center where they overlap.

The 10–20 year class (the heart of the matter)

Densely stocked young hardwoods or mixed stands where stems are roughly wrist- to arm-thickness. Understory cover that lets a hen with chicks move through but stops a hawk's stoop. Rhododendron, mountain laurel, fruit brambles, and blueberry are signals.

The 5–15 year class (early successional)

Brushy seedling/sapling stands provide brood habitat. Field edges and openings with herbaceous cover support the insect populations young birds need for their high-energy first weeks. In Tennessee, this stage is the most scarce — and the most actively managed for on RGS focal areas.

The 25+ year class (mast + drumming)

Mature, predominantly deciduous forest with oak, hickory, and beech provides hard mast through fall and winter. Drumming logs — typically 10"+ diameter downed trunks — get used year after year as display sites in spring.

Borrowed lessons from other grouse states

Minnesota — the activity center concept. Northern range research frames grouse habitat as three age classes (0–10 years, 10–25 years, 25+ years) that must all sit within a single home range. The point where the three age classes intersect is called an activity center. The takeaway for TN scouting: don't just find young forest — find young forest that touches both early successional cover and mature mast trees. Activity centers are where the bird actually lives.

Vermont — winter softwoods matter more than expected. In northern range, grouse dive into snow roosts on cold nights. In Vermont and points south, when snow cover is lacking, the birds shift to softwood stands — hemlock, spruce, pine — for protective cover and insulation. Tennessee winters rarely deliver deep persistent snow, so this matters here too: shortleaf pine pockets and hemlock draws adjacent to hardwood feeding areas are likely winter refuges. Worth noting on scouting maps.

How to read the ground

Walk edges where age classes meet — particularly where a 10–20 year stand meets a 25+ stand near water. Walk logging roads slowly. Stop walking sometimes to listen (TJH tip): grouse give themselves away with peeps and quiet movement when a hunter goes still, and the pause itself makes held birds antsy. Drumming runs mid-April through mid-May and is the single best indicator of a holding population.

§ 05 — Places

Where to put boots down.

Four candidate areas on or near the Cumberland Plateau, sorted by current promise. All within 2–3 hours of Nashville.

Author's pick

If you've got one weekend, go to Catoosa.

Five thousand acres of Plateau hardwoods, contiguous with another four thousand at Lone Mountain, and an active Ruffed Grouse Society burn-and-cut project working the young-forest deficit. Forum reports of birds in Lancing just north. 2h 20m from Nashville. This is the spot to start.

№ 01 — Top Pick

Catoosa WMA

5,000 ac · Cumberland Plateau · 2h 20m from Nashville

Adjacent to Lone Mountain State Forest (~9,000 acres of contiguous habitat together). Hosts a Ruffed Grouse Society habitat burn-and-cut project started in 2022. Forum reports of birds nearby in Lancing.

Why it ranks first: active young-forest management is the single best predictor of grouse on the Plateau, and Catoosa has the most of it currently underway.

№ 02

Lone Mountain State Forest

4,000 ac · Cumberland Plateau · 2h 45m from Nashville

Contiguous with Catoosa. Same RGS Dynamic Forest Restoration Block project. Treat as one hunt area with Catoosa; cross-reference unit boundaries before opening day.

№ 03

Standing Stone SF & WMA

11,000 ac · Cumberland Plateau · 2h 10m from Nashville

Heavy historical timber cutting and fire have created a mixed-age mosaic. By stand age: 38% in 81+ years, 36% in 61–80 years, 11% in 41–60, 7% in 21–40, 7% under 20 years. The under-20 fraction is small but present — find where it abuts older stands.

Status: grouse presence unconfirmed; promising on paper, needs ground-truthing.

№ 04

Pickett SF & WMA

8,000 ac · Cumberland Plateau · 2h 45m from Nashville

Northern Plateau, near the Kentucky line. Needs scouting; potentially holds birds based on habitat type and adjacency to managed lands. Lowest data confidence of the four.

§ 06 — Season & Tactics

What the regs say, what the woods say.

Season (2025–26)

October 11, 2025 – February 28, 2026. Daily bag limit: 3 birds. Statewide.

License

TN resident annual hunting license required. Confirm current pricing and any WMA-specific permits on the TWRA site before going.

Best practical tactics

  • Walk the edges where age classes meet. Pure mature timber holds drummers but not flushes.
  • Pause every 30–40 yards. Going still pressures held birds to move and reveal themselves with peeps. (Credit: TJH.)
  • Logging roads in early morning for gravel & grit — birds work road edges at first light.
  • Listen for drumming in spring to scout for next fall. April–May drum sites = November cover.
